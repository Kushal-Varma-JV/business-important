Chapter 3 · Business Environment

9 marks: two 4-mark questions + one 1-mark question. One 4-mark question must be on features.

4 marks⭐ guaranteed1. Features of Business Environment

Business environment means the sum total of all individuals, institutions and other forces that are outside the control of a business enterprise but that may affect its performance.
In simple words: "Take the universe, subtract the organisation, and what remains is the environment."
  1. Totality of external forces: It is the sum total of all things outside the business firm, so it is aggregative in nature.
  2. Specific and general forces: Specific forces (investors, customers, competitors, suppliers) affect a firm directly in its day-to-day work. General forces (social, political, legal, technological conditions) affect all firms, and so affect one firm only indirectly.
  3. Inter-relatedness: The different parts of the environment are closely linked. E.g. longer life expectancy and health awareness increased demand for fat-free cooking oil and health resorts, and these new products then changed people's lifestyles.
  4. Dynamic nature: It keeps changing: new technology, shifts in consumer preferences, entry of new competitors.
  5. Uncertainty: It is very difficult to predict future happenings, especially in fast-changing fields like information technology and fashion.
  6. Complexity: It is made of many inter-related, changing forces, so it is easy to understand in parts but hard to grasp as a whole. E.g. it is hard to say how much social vs economic factors changed the demand for a product.
  7. Relativity: It differs from country to country and even region to region. E.g. demand for sarees is high in India but almost nil in France.

4 marks2. Dimensions of Business Environment ("Which dimension?")

The dimensions are the general forces that make up the business environment. There are five: Economic, Social, Technological, Political and Legal.
EconomicSocialTechnologicalPoliticalLegal
  1. Economic environment: Interest rates, inflation rates, disposable income of people, stock market indices and the value of the rupee. E.g. low interest rates increase spending on homes and cars bought on loans. High inflation raises the cost of raw materials and wages.
  2. Social environment: Customs and traditions, values, social trends and society's expectations from business. E.g. Diwali, Eid, Christmas and Guru Parv create demand for sweets and greeting cards. The health-and-fitness trend created demand for organic food, gyms and mineral water.
  3. Technological environment: Scientific improvements and innovations that give new ways of producing goods and services and new methods of operating a business. E.g. online railway ticket booking, and the shift from typewriters to computers.
  4. Political environment: Political stability and peace in the country, and the attitude of the government towards business. Stability builds investor confidence. Political unrest creates uncertainty.
  5. Legal environment: Laws passed by the government, administrative orders, court judgments and decisions of commissions at centre, state and local levels. E.g. the warning "Cigarette smoking is injurious to health". Baby-food adverts must say mother's milk is best. Relevant Acts include the Companies Act 2013, the Consumer Protection Act 1986 and the Competition Act 2002.

4 marks3. Importance of Business Environment (backup topic)

The guaranteed question is on features, but learn these 6 points too in case the second question asks for importance.
  1. Identifying opportunities and getting the first mover advantage: Spotting positive trends early lets a firm use them before competitors. E.g. Maruti led the small car market because it first saw the need, given rising petrol prices and a large middle class.
  2. Identifying threats and early warning signals: E.g. if a foreign MNC is entering India with substitutes, an Indian firm can prepare by improving quality, cutting costs and advertising more.
  3. Tapping useful resources: The environment supplies inputs (finance, machines, raw materials, labour) and takes outputs (goods, taxes, returns). Understanding it helps the firm get these resources.
  4. Coping with rapid changes: Markets, technology and competition change fast. Studying the environment helps managers develop suitable responses.
  5. Assisting in planning and policy formulation: Opportunities and threats become the basis for future plans and policies. E.g. new competitors force a firm to rethink its plans.
  6. Improving performance: Firms that keep monitoring their environment improve present performance and succeed for longer.

Chapter 2 · Principles of Management

The 4-mark question comes from Taylor's principles, Taylor's techniques, or Fayol's principles.

4 marks4. Taylor's Principles of Scientific Management

F.W. Taylor (1856–1915) was an American mechanical engineer, known as the 'Father of Scientific Management'.
Scientific management means conducting business with standardised tools, methods and trained people to increase output, improve quality and reduce costs and waste. In Taylor's words, it is "knowing exactly what you want men to do and seeing that they do it in the best and cheapest way."
  1. Science, not rule of thumb: Instead of trial-and-error (rule of thumb), find the one best way to do each job through study and analysis (work-study), and use it throughout the organisation. Even loading pig iron can be scientifically planned.
  2. Harmony, not discord: There should be complete harmony between management and workers, not class conflict. This needs a mental revolution in both: management shares gains with workers, and workers work hard and accept change. E.g. Japanese workers on strike wear a black badge but work extra hours.
  3. Cooperation, not individualism: An extension of "harmony, not discord." Competition is replaced by cooperation. Management welcomes and rewards workers' suggestions and consults them on important decisions. Workers avoid strikes and unreasonable demands. Work and responsibility are divided almost equally.
  4. Development of each and every person to his or her greatest efficiency and prosperity: Select workers scientifically, give work that suits their physical, mental and intellectual abilities, and train them. Efficient workers produce more and earn more.
  5. Maximum, not restricted output: Management and workers should work together to maximise output and increase the surplus, instead of restricting output and quarrelling over shares.
⚠️ The chapter explains only the first 4 principles in detail, but its Summary lists 5. Always write all 5.
⭐ Mental revolution: A change in the attitude of workers and management towards each other, from competition to cooperation. Both realise they need each other and aim to increase the surplus.

4 marks5. Techniques of Scientific Management

Techniques are the practical methods Taylor developed through his experiments to put his principles into action.
  1. Functional foremanship: Taylor separated planning from execution at the shop floor. Since no single foreman has all the qualities needed, he proposed 8 specialist foremen:
    • Under the Planning Incharge: Instruction card clerk (drafts instructions), Route clerk (decides the route of production), Time and cost clerk (prepares time and cost sheets), Disciplinarian (ensures discipline).
    • Under the Production Incharge: Speed boss (timely, accurate completion), Gang boss (keeps machines and tools ready), Repair boss (keeps machines in working condition), Inspector (checks quality).
    It extends division of work and specialisation to the shop floor.
  2. Standardisation and simplification of work: Standardisation means setting standards for every activity: process, raw material, time, product, machinery, methods, working conditions. Simplification means eliminating unnecessary varieties, sizes and dimensions. This saves costs, reduces inventory and makes fuller use of equipment.
  3. Method study: Finds the one best way of doing a job, from buying raw materials to delivering the final product. It aims to minimise cost and maximise quality and customer satisfaction. Taylor's assembly line idea came from method study and was used by Ford.
  4. Motion study: Studies body movements (lifting, sitting, changing positions) to remove unnecessary motions. Motions are classified as productive, incidental or unproductive. E.g. Frank Gilbreth reduced bricklaying motions from 18 to 5.
  5. Time study: Fixes the standard time for a well-defined job. It helps decide the number of workers needed, design incentive schemes and fix labour costs. E.g. 20 minutes per box → 3 boxes an hour → 21 boxes in a 7-hour working day.
  6. Fatigue study: Decides the amount and frequency of rest intervals so workers regain energy. Causes of fatigue include long working hours, unsuitable work, poor relations with the boss and bad working conditions.
  7. Differential piece wage system: Different wage rates for efficient and inefficient workers. E.g. standard = 10 units a day: ₹50/unit at or above standard, ₹40/unit below. 11 units = ₹550, but 9 units = ₹360. The ₹190 gap motivates inefficient workers to improve.
Functional foremanship: the worker gets orders from 8 specialists (4 planning + 4 production).

4 marks6. Fayol's 14 Principles of Management

Henri Fayol (1841–1925) was a French mining engineer, known as the 'Father of General Management'. His book Administration Industrielle et Générale (1917) was published in English as General and Industrial Management (1949).
If the question says "any 8", write 8 headings with one line each. If it says "explain any 4", write 4 with an example each. Learn all 14 so you can do either.
  1. Division of work: Work is divided into small tasks, each done by a trained specialist. It produces "more and better work for the same effort". E.g. separate finance, marketing and production departments.
  2. Authority and responsibility: Authority is the right to give orders and obtain obedience. Responsibility is its corollary. There must be a balance between the two. E.g. a sales manager needs authority to offer 60 days' credit to close the deal, but is given only 40 days.
  3. Discipline: Obedience to organisational rules and the employment agreement. It requires good superiors, clear and fair agreements, and judicious penalties. E.g. both union and management honour their agreement.
  4. Unity of command: Each employee should receive orders from only one boss. Dual subordination causes confusion. E.g. marketing allows 10% discount but finance says 5%.
  5. Unity of direction: Each group of activities with the same objective must have one head and one plan. E.g. a company making cars and motorcycles should have two separate divisions.
  6. Subordination of individual interest to general interest: The organisation's interest comes before any individual's. E.g. a manager must not misuse power for personal or family benefit.
  7. Remuneration of employees: Pay should be fair to both employees (a reasonable standard of living) and the organisation (within its paying capacity), i.e. just and equitable.
  8. Centralisation and decentralisation: There should be a balance between concentrating decision-making authority (centralisation) and spreading it (decentralisation). E.g. panchayats got more powers to spend funds.
  9. Scalar chain: The formal line of authority from the highest to the lowest rank, which communication should follow. In an emergency, a gang plank allows direct contact between people at the same level (E can contact O directly).
  10. Order: "A place for everything (everyone) and everything (everyone) in its place." People and materials must be in suitable places at the appropriate time.
  11. Equity: Kindliness and justice towards all workers. No discrimination on the basis of sex, religion, language, caste, belief or nationality.
  12. Stability of personnel: Employee turnover should be minimised. After rigorous selection, employees should get a minimum fixed tenure to show results.
  13. Initiative: Workers should be encouraged to develop and carry out their own plans for improvement, e.g. through an employee suggestion system with rewards.
  14. Esprit de corps: Management should promote team spirit, unity and harmony. A manager should replace 'I' with 'We'.
⚠️ The textbook prints this as "Espirit de corps". The correct spelling is Esprit de corps (French for team spirit).

Chapter 4 · Planning

Planning is deciding in advance what to do and how to do it. It bridges the gap between where we are and where we want to go. The 4-mark question comes from features, limitations, or the planning process.

4 marks7. Features of Planning

Definition: Planning is setting objectives for a given time period, formulating various courses of action to achieve them, and selecting the best possible alternative from among them.
  1. Planning focuses on achieving objectives: It is purposeful. It has no meaning unless it helps achieve predetermined organisational goals.
  2. Planning is a primary function of management: It lays the base for all other functions (organising, staffing, directing, controlling), which are performed within the framework of plans. This is called the primacy of planning.
  3. Planning is pervasive: It is needed at all levels and in all departments. Top management plans for the whole organisation, middle management for departments, and supervisors for day-to-day operations.
  4. Planning is continuous: A plan is made for a period (month, quarter, year). When it ends, a new plan is needed. Plan → implement → new plan (the planning cycle).
  5. Planning is futuristic: It looks ahead and is based on forecasting. E.g. a sales forecast is the basis of the annual production plan.
  6. Planning involves decision making: It means choosing the best among alternatives. If there is only one option, no planning is needed.
  7. Planning is a mental exercise: It needs foresight, imagination and sound judgement. It is thinking before doing, and must be logical and systematic, not guesswork.

4 marks8. Limitations of Planning

  1. Planning leads to rigidity: Once a plan is fixed, managers may not be able to change it even when circumstances change. Following an outdated plan may harm the organisation.
  2. Planning may not work in a dynamic environment: Economic, political, physical, legal and social conditions keep changing. Policy changes, political instability, natural calamities or competition can upset plans, because planning cannot foresee everything.
  3. Planning reduces creativity: Plans are made by top management, and others just carry them out. They cannot deviate or act on their own, so their initiative and creativity are lost.
  4. Planning involves huge costs: Time and money go into collecting facts, calculations, boardroom meetings, expert discussions and preliminary investigations. Sometimes the costs exceed the benefits.
  5. Planning is a time-consuming process: Sometimes making the plan takes so long that little time is left to implement it.
  6. Planning does not guarantee success: Success depends on proper implementation. Relying on plans that worked before can create a false sense of security and lead to failure.
Despite its limitations, planning is not useless. It is a tool to be used with caution.

4 marks9. Steps in the Planning Process

1 Setting objectives→ 2 Developing premises→ 3 Identifying alternatives→ 4 Evaluating alternatives→ 5 Selecting an alternative→ 6 Implementing the plan→ 7 Follow-up action
  1. Setting objectives: The first step. Decide what the organisation wants to achieve (e.g. increase sales by 20%). Objectives must be clear and should percolate down to every department and employee.
  2. Developing premises: Premises are assumptions about the future (e.g. forecasts of demand, interest rates, tax rates). All planners must use the same assumptions.
  3. Identifying alternative courses of action: List all the ways the objective can be achieved, whether routine or innovative.
  4. Evaluating alternative courses: Weigh the pros and cons of each alternative in terms of feasibility and consequences (e.g. risk vs return).
  5. Selecting an alternative: The real point of decision making. Choose the most feasible, profitable option with the fewest negative consequences. Sometimes a combination is chosen.
  6. Implementing the plan: Put the plan into action, e.g. hire more labour and buy machinery to increase production. Other functions of management come in here.
  7. Follow-up action: Monitor whether the plan is being implemented on schedule, to ensure objectives are achieved.

Chapter 8 · Controlling

Controlling means ensuring that activities in an organisation are performed as per the plans. It is goal-oriented and pervasive (needed at all levels and in all organisations). The 4-mark question comes from importance, limitations, or the controlling process.

4 marks10. Importance of Controlling

Koontz and O'Donnell: "Managerial control implies the measurement of accomplishment against the standard and the correction of deviations to assure attainment of objectives according to plans."
Controlling is not the last function. It brings the management cycle back to planning.
  1. Accomplishing organisational goals: Measures progress towards goals, brings deviations to light and indicates corrective action, keeping the organisation on the right track.
  2. Judging accuracy of standards: Helps management check whether the standards set are accurate and objective, and revise them as the organisation and environment change.
  3. Making efficient use of resources: Each activity follows predetermined standards, which reduces wastage and spoilage of resources.
  4. Improving employee motivation: Employees know in advance what is expected and on what standards they will be appraised, so they perform better.
  5. Ensuring order and discipline: Keeping a close check on activities minimises dishonest behaviour. E.g. computer monitoring caught two employees stealing from an import-export firm.
  6. Facilitating coordination in action: Every department follows predetermined, well-coordinated standards, so all efforts move towards the overall goals.

4 marks⭐ asked 4 times11. Limitations of Controlling

  1. Difficulty in setting quantitative standards: Control loses effectiveness when standards cannot be expressed in numbers. E.g. employee morale, job satisfaction and human behaviour are hard to measure.
  2. Little control on external factors: An enterprise generally cannot control external factors such as government policies, technological changes and competition.
  3. Resistance from employees: Employees see control as a restriction on their freedom. E.g. they may object to being watched on CCTV.
  4. Costly affair: Control needs a lot of money, time and effort. A small enterprise may not be able to afford an expensive control system. Its cost should not exceed its benefits.

4 marks12. Steps in the Controlling Process

1 Setting performance standards→ 2 Measuring actual performance→ 3 Comparing with standards→ 4 Analysing deviations→ 5 Taking corrective action
  1. Setting performance standards: Standards are the benchmarks against which performance is measured. They can be quantitative (cost, revenue, units, time) or qualitative (goodwill, motivation). E.g. reduce defects from 10 to 5 per 1,000 pieces. Standards should be flexible.
  2. Measurement of actual performance: Measure objectively, using personal observation, sample checking or performance reports. Use the same units as the standards, and measure during the work where possible.
  3. Comparing actual performance with standards: Reveals the deviation between actual and desired results. This is easier when standards are quantitative.
  4. Analysing deviations: Fix an acceptable range of deviation and focus on key areas using critical point control and management by exception. Find the causes: unrealistic standards, defective process, inadequate resources, structural drawbacks, organisational constraints, or environmental factors.
  5. Taking corrective action: Needed only when deviations go beyond the acceptable range. E.g. training employees, adding workers and equipment, or allowing overtime. If the deviation cannot be corrected, revise the standards.
If the question asks for the "first four steps", write steps 1–4 only. Critical point control and management by exception are explained on the Tier 3 page.

✍️ Which term is used for the difference between standard and actual performance? (1 mark) Deviation.
✍️ Bhanu Prasad fell short of his daily target by 10 units for two days. His manager wants him dismissed. Which principle should the CEO consider? Management by exception: only significant deviations beyond the acceptable limit need top management's attention. A small shortfall over two days is minor, so dismissal is not justified.
✍️ "Planning is looking ahead and controlling is looking back." Comment. Only partly true. Planning looks ahead because it is based on forecasts, but it is also guided by past experience. Controlling looks back at past performance, but its corrective action improves future performance. So both look ahead and back.
