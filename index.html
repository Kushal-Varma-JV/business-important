Chapter 6 · Staffing

Highest-mark chapter: one 8-mark + one 4-mark question.

8 marks1. Steps in the Staffing Process

Staffing is the managerial function of filling and keeping filled the positions in the organisation structure. In simple words, it is "putting people to jobs", i.e. finding the right person for the right job.
1 Estimate manpower→ 2 Recruitment→ 3 Selection→ 4 Placement & Orientation→ 5 Training & Development→ 6 Performance Appraisal→ 7 Promotion & Career Planning→ 8 Compensation
  1. Estimating manpower requirements: Find out how many and what type of people are needed. Workload analysis shows the number and type of people required. Workforce analysis shows the number and type available. Comparing the two tells us whether we are understaffed, overstaffed or optimally staffed. The needs are then written down as a job description and candidate profile.
  2. Recruitment: The process of searching for prospective employees and stimulating them to apply. It creates a pool of candidates from internal and external sources (e.g. advertisements).
  3. Selection: Choosing the best person from the pool through tests and interviews. It ensures the organisation gets the best candidate and raises the self-esteem of those selected.
  4. Placement and Orientation: Orientation means introducing the new employee to superiors, colleagues and subordinates and familiarising him with the rules and policies. Placement means the employee occupies the post for which he was selected.
  5. Training and Development: Helps employees learn, improve their skills and grow in their careers. Trained, motivated staff perform better, and the organisation can attract and retain talent.
  6. Performance Appraisal: Evaluating an employee's current or past performance against predetermined standards and giving feedback.
  7. Promotion and Career Planning: Placing employees in positions of higher responsibility, usually with more pay and job satisfaction. Serves employees' long-term interests.
  8. Compensation: All forms of pay or rewards given to employees. Direct: wages, salaries, incentives, commissions, bonus. Indirect: employer-paid insurance, vacations. Pay may be time-based (daily, monthly) or performance-based (piece rate).
The textbook gives steps 1–5 first and adds steps 6–8 for small organisations, where the line manager does all the work. For an 8-mark answer, write all 8 steps.
🧠 Memory trick: "Every Recruit Should Probably Train, Perform, Progress, Collect pay"
Estimate → Recruit → Select → Place → Train → Performance appraisal → Promotion → Compensation
📌 Case lines
  • "One department has more staff than work" → Estimating manpower (overstaffing). The fix is transfer or removal.
  • "The new employee was introduced to colleagues and told the company rules" → Orientation
  • "Her work was compared with the set standards and she was given feedback" → Performance appraisal
  • "A steel company with 800 staff needs 200 more" → Recruitment is needed (start with estimating manpower)
✍️ Explain the steps in the staffing process. (8 marks) Write all 8 steps with 1–2 lines each: estimating manpower requirements (workload and workforce analysis), recruitment, selection, placement and orientation, training and development, performance appraisal, promotion and career planning, compensation.
✍️ What is the difference between orientation and placement? (2 marks) Orientation means introducing the new employee to other employees and familiarising him with the rules and policies. Placement means the employee occupies the post for which he was selected.
✍️ What is workload analysis and workforce analysis? Workload analysis assesses the number and type of people needed to do the jobs. Workforce analysis shows the number and type of people available. Together they show whether the organisation is understaffed or overstaffed.

8 marks2. External Sources of Recruitment (learn any 8)

Recruitment is the process of searching for prospective employees and stimulating them to apply for jobs in the organisation.
There are two sources: Internal (transfers and promotions) and External (from outside the organisation).
  1. Direct Recruitment: A notice is put on the notice board. Job-seekers gather outside the premises on the given date and are selected on the spot. Used for unskilled or semi-skilled casual ('badli') workers paid daily wages. It is very inexpensive (no advertising cost) and suits a rush of work or when permanent workers are absent.
  2. Casual Callers: Reputed companies keep a database of unsolicited applicants and screen it when vacancies arise. It reduces recruitment cost.
  3. Advertisement: Vacancies advertised in newspapers or trade and professional journals. Used when a wider choice is needed and for most senior positions. More information about the job can be given. Drawback: a flood of applications, many unsuitable.
  4. Employment Exchange: Run by the Government. Good for unskilled and skilled operative jobs. In some cases the law makes it compulsory to notify vacancies to it. It links job-seekers and employers. Drawback: records are often not up to date.
  5. Placement Agencies and Management Consultants: Agencies collect bio-data of many candidates and recommend suitable names for a fee. Useful where extensive screening is needed. Consultants specialise in middle and top-level, technical and professional posts and can attract top executives from other companies.
  6. Campus Recruitment: Recruiting from colleges and institutes of management and technology for technical, professional and managerial jobs.
  7. Recommendations of Employees: Candidates introduced by present employees or their friends and relatives. Their background is known, so a kind of preliminary screening has already happened.
  8. Labour Contractors: Supply unskilled workers at short notice. Drawback: if the contractor leaves, all workers hired through him may leave too.
  9. Advertising on Television: Vacancies, job requirements and the company profile are telecast.
  10. Web Publishing: Job websites provide information about job-seekers and job openings. They are used by both candidates and companies.
🧠 Memory trick: Think of where a job-seeker could hear about a job, from cheapest to modern: notice board (direct) → walk-ins (casual callers) → newspaper (advertisement) → govt office (exchange) → agency → college (campus) → friend (recommendation) → contractor → TV → internet.
📌 Case lines
  • "Urgent festival order, extra workers needed for a few days" → Direct recruitment (casual or 'badli' workers)
  • "Portal like www.naukaripao.com for senior jobs" → Web publishing
  • "Company visits IIMs or engineering colleges" → Campus recruitment
  • "Employees rewarded for referring candidates" → Recommendations of employees
  • "Government office matches job-seekers with employers" → Employment exchange
✍️ Explain any 8 external sources of recruitment. (8 marks) Pick any 8 from the list above and write 1–2 lines each. The safest 8 are: direct recruitment, casual callers, advertisement, employment exchange, placement agencies and management consultants, campus recruitment, recommendations of employees, web publishing.
✍️ A paper-plate company gets an urgent festival order for 50,000 extra plates. Which method of recruitment should it use? Direct recruitment. A notice is put on the notice board, casual workers gather and are selected on the spot. It is quick and inexpensive and suits a temporary rush of work.
✍️ Kaul Consultants launched www.naukaripao.com for senior professionals. Name the source and state 4 benefits. Source: Web publishing (an external source). Benefits of external sources: (1) Qualified personnel, because trained people can be attracted. (2) Wider choice, because many people apply. (3) Fresh talent, because new blood enters the organisation. (4) Competitive spirit, because existing staff work harder to compete with outsiders.

8 marks3. Steps in the Selection Process

Selection is the process of identifying and choosing the best person out of a number of prospective candidates for a job. At every stage, some candidates are eliminated until the right person is found.
1 Preliminary screening→ 2 Selection tests→ 3 Interview→ 4 Reference checks→ 5 Selection decision→ 6 Medical exam→ 7 Job offer→ 8 Contract of employment

A candidate who fails at any step is rejected and does not move to the next step.

  1. Preliminary Screening: Unqualified or unfit applicants are eliminated using the application form. Preliminary interviews reject misfits for reasons not visible in the form.
  2. Selection Tests: Tests that measure a person's characteristics:
    • Intelligence test: measures IQ, i.e. learning ability and the ability to make decisions and judgements.
    • Aptitude test: measures the potential to learn new skills (capacity to develop).
    • Personality test: gives clues to emotions, reactions, maturity and values. Difficult to design.
    • Trade test: measures existing skills and proficiency.
    • Interest test: shows a person's pattern of interests.
  3. Employment Interview: A formal, in-depth conversation to judge the applicant's suitability. Today both sides seek information.
  4. Reference and Background Checks: References (previous employers, teachers, professors) are contacted to verify information and learn more about the applicant.
  5. Selection Decision: The final choice is made from those who passed the tests, interview and checks. The concerned manager's views are considered, because he will be responsible for the new employee.
  6. Medical Examination: After the selection decision and before the job offer, the candidate takes a medical fitness test.
  7. Job Offer: Made through a letter of appointment that states the date of joining, with reasonable time to report.
  8. Contract of Employment: Documents such as the attestation form and a written contract are signed. The contract covers job title, duties, pay, allowances, hours, leave rules, grievance and disciplinary procedure, and termination.
🧠 Memory trick: "Some Tough Interviewers Really Demand Medical OK Certificates"
Screening → Tests → Interview → References → Decision → Medical → Offer → Contract
Tests: "I A P T I" = Intelligence, Aptitude, Personality, Trade, Interest
📌 Case lines
  • "Security firm needs reliable people who won't leak client secrets" → Reference and background checks (plus personality test)
  • "Test checks how quickly she can learn new software" → Aptitude test
  • "Test checks how well he can already weld" → Trade test
  • "Candidate sent for a fitness check before the appointment letter" → Medical examination
✍️ Explain the stages in the selection process. (8 marks) Write the 8 steps with 1–2 lines each: preliminary screening, selection tests (name the 5 tests), employment interview, reference and background checks, selection decision, medical examination, job offer, contract of employment.
✍️ Difference between aptitude test and trade test? (2 marks) An aptitude test measures the potential to acquire new skills. A trade test measures the actual skills a person already has.

8 marks4. Methods of Training (4 On-the-Job + 4 Off-the-Job)

Training is any process by which the aptitudes, skills and abilities of employees to perform specific jobs are increased.
On-the-job methods are used at the workplace while working (learning while doing). Off-the-job methods are used away from the workplace (learning before doing).

🏭 On-the-Job Methods

  1. Apprenticeship Programmes: The trainee works under the guidance of a master worker to gain a high level of skill. Used for skilled trades like plumbers, electricians and iron-workers. Everyone gets a uniform period of training, so slow learners may need extra training.
  2. Coaching: The superior acts as a coach. He sets mutually agreed goals, suggests how to achieve them, reviews progress and suggests changes. The trainee is often being groomed to replace the senior manager.
  3. Internship Training: A joint programme between educational institutions and business firms. Students carry on their studies and also work in a factory or office to gain practical knowledge.
  4. Job Rotation: The trainee is shifted from one department or job to another. This gives a broad understanding of the whole business, builds cooperation between departments and makes promotions and transfers easier.

🏫 Off-the-Job Methods

  1. Classroom Lectures / Conferences: Good for conveying specific information such as rules, procedures and methods. Audio-visuals make them interesting.
  2. Films: Show skills that other methods can't easily demonstrate. Very effective when followed by discussion.
  3. Case Study: Real problems faced by managers. Trainees identify the problem, analyse causes, develop solutions and choose the best one.
  4. Computer Modelling: A computer simulates the work environment, so trainees learn without the risk or high cost of real mistakes.
  5. Vestibule Training: Employees learn on the same equipment they will use, but in a separate classroom away from the work floor. Used for sophisticated machinery.
  6. Programmed Instruction: Information is broken into small units arranged from simple to complex. The trainee answers questions or fills blanks.
🧠 Memory trick: On-the-job: "A Cool Intern's Job" = Apprenticeship, Coaching, Internship, Job rotation.
Off-the-job: "Class Films Can Compute Very Precisely" = Classroom, Films, Case study, Computer modelling, Vestibule, Programmed instruction.
📌 Case lines
  • "Workers remain idle because they don't know the hi-tech machines; engineer visits are costly" → Vestibule training
  • "Trainee electrician works for 2 years under a senior electrician" → Apprenticeship
  • "Trainee manager moves through sales, finance and production for 3 months each" → Job rotation
  • "College student works in a company during the course" → Internship
  • "Pilot practises on a simulator" → Computer modelling
✍️ Explain any 4 on-the-job and 4 off-the-job training methods. (8 marks) On-the-job: apprenticeship, coaching, internship, job rotation. Off-the-job: classroom lectures, films, case study, vestibule training (or computer modelling, programmed instruction). Write 1–2 lines on each.
✍️ Distinguish between on-the-job and off-the-job methods. (2 marks) On-the-job methods are used at the workplace while the employee is working (learning while doing). Off-the-job methods are used away from the workplace (learning before doing).

8 marks5. Meaning and Importance of Organising

Organising is the process of defining and grouping the activities of the enterprise and establishing authority relationships among them. (Theo Haimann)
In other words, it is the process that starts the implementation of plans by clarifying jobs and working relationships and using resources effectively to reach the desired goals.
⭐ "Define organising" has been asked 4 times, so learn this definition word for word.

Importance of Organising

  1. Benefits of specialisation: Work is allocated systematically. Doing the same task repeatedly builds experience and specialisation, which reduces workload and increases productivity.
  2. Clarity in working relationships: Makes clear who reports to whom. This removes confusion in passing information and instructions and helps fix responsibility.
  3. Optimum utilisation of resources: Proper assignment of jobs avoids overlapping and duplication of work, so material, financial and human resources are used best with minimum wastage.
  4. Adaptation to change: The structure can be modified to suit changes in the business environment. This gives the enterprise stability so it can survive and grow.
  5. Effective administration: A clear description of jobs and duties avoids confusion and duplication, making management easier and more effective.
  6. Development of personnel: Through delegation, managers pass routine work to subordinates. Managers get time to innovate, and subordinates learn to handle challenges and realise their potential.
  7. Expansion and growth: Helps the enterprise add new job positions, departments, product lines and territories, which increases its customer base, sales and profit.

  • "No two people were doing the same work, so nothing was wasted" → Optimum utilisation of resources

8 marks6. Delegation: Meaning, Elements and Importance

Delegation refers to the downward transfer of authority from a superior to a subordinate.
⚠️ Delegation does not mean abdication (giving up). The manager remains accountable for the work, and the authority can be taken back and given to someone else.

Elements of Delegation (Louis Allen)

  1. Authority: The right to command subordinates and take decisions within the scope of one's position. It arises from the formal position and flows downward. It is highest at the top and decreases as we go down. It is limited by laws and the organisation's rules.
  2. Responsibility: The obligation of a subordinate to properly perform the assigned duty. It arises from the superior–subordinate relationship and flows upward.
  3. Accountability: Answerability for the final outcome. It cannot be delegated and flows upward. It is enforced through regular feedback.

⭐ Golden line: "Authority is delegated, responsibility is assumed, accountability is imposed."
Authority must be equal to (commensurate with) responsibility. Too much authority can lead to misuse. Too much responsibility without authority makes a person ineffective.

Importance of Delegation

  1. Effective management: Managers are freed from routine work and get time for important matters and new areas.
  2. Employee development: Employees get chances to use their talent, develop skills and become better leaders and future managers.
  3. Motivation of employees: Being trusted with responsibility builds self-esteem and confidence, so the employee tries to perform better.
  4. Facilitation of growth: Provides a ready team of trained, experienced employees to lead new ventures and branches.
  5. Basis of management hierarchy: Creates superior–subordinate relationships. The flow of authority decides who reports to whom.
  6. Better coordination: Authority, responsibility and accountability define powers, duties and answerability, which avoids overlapping and duplication.
🧠 Memory trick: "Every Employee Must Grow Higher Constantly"
Effective management, Employee development, Motivation, Growth, Hierarchy, Coordination
📌 Case lines

  • "Manager says: 'Even though you did the work, I will answer to the MD'" → Accountability cannot be delegated
  • "All files must be approved by me", so work piles up → No delegation, which causes delays
✍️ Explain the meaning, elements and importance of delegation. (8 marks) Meaning (downward transfer of authority, not abdication). Then the 3 elements: authority, responsibility, accountability. Then any 4–6 importance points: effective management, employee development, motivation, growth, basis of hierarchy, better coordination.

No. For effective delegation, authority must match responsibility. The foreman was given responsibility without the authority needed to do the job, so he cannot be held fully responsible.

8 marks7. Decentralisation: Meaning and Importance

Decentralisation refers to the delegation of authority throughout all levels of the organisation. Decision-making authority is pushed down the chain of command, close to the point of action.
Centralised = decision-making authority stays with top management. Decentralised = it is shared with lower levels.
No organisation is completely centralised or decentralised. A balance is needed. Decentralisation is an optional policy of top management.

Importance of Decentralisation

  1. Develops initiative among subordinates: Freedom to decide builds self-reliance and confidence. It also helps identify executives who can become dynamic leaders.
  2. Develops managerial talent for the future: Handling assignments independently gives experience. This creates a pool of qualified people for promotions and also shows who cannot handle more responsibility.
  3. Quick decision making: Decisions are taken near the point of action without approval from many levels, so they are faster and information is less likely to be distorted.
  4. Relief to top management: Less direct supervision is needed, so top managers get more time for important policy decisions.
  5. Facilitates growth: Departments get autonomy and compete with each other, which raises productivity and earns more money for expansion.
  6. Better control: Performance of each level and department can be evaluated separately and they can be held accountable. Tools like the balance score card and MIS are used.
⚠️ Caution: Too much decentralisation can lead to disintegration if departments follow their own rules. Major policy decisions should stay centralised.
🧠 Memory trick: "In Mumbai, Queues Rarely Get Cleared"
Initiative, Managerial talent, Quick decisions, Relief to top management, Growth, Control
📌 Case lines
  • "Wipro moved from centralised to decentralised management; each business head given growth responsibility" → Decentralisation
  • "Directors take even minor decisions, company losing market share to MNCs" → Problem: over-centralisation. Solution: decentralise (and use a divisional structure).
  • "Branch managers can decide local discounts without asking head office" → Decentralisation (quick decision making)
✍️ What is decentralisation? Explain its importance. (8 marks) Meaning (delegation at all levels; authority pushed down). Then the 6 points: initiative, managerial talent, quick decision making, relief to top management, growth, better control. End with the caution line.
✍️ "Decentralisation is extending delegation to the lowest level." Comment. True. Delegation is between a superior and his immediate subordinate. Decentralisation is the systematic delegation of authority at every level down to the lowest, except authority that must stay at the centre.

Chapter 7 · Directing

Directing means instructing, guiding, counselling, motivating and leading people to achieve objectives. Its 4 elements are Supervision, Motivation, Leadership and Communication.

8 marks8. Principles of Directing

  1. Maximum individual contribution: Directing techniques should help every individual contribute to his maximum potential. For example, a good reward plan brings out untapped energy.
  2. Harmony of objectives: Individual goals (e.g. a good salary) and organisational goals (e.g. productivity, profit) may conflict. Good directing shows that they support each other.
  3. Unity of command: A person should receive instructions from one superior only. Orders from more than one boss cause confusion, conflict and disorder.
  4. Appropriateness of direction technique: Use motivation and leadership methods that suit the subordinate's needs, abilities and attitudes. Money motivates some people, promotion motivates others.
  5. Managerial communication: Instructions must be clear at all levels, and feedback should confirm that the subordinate has understood.
  6. Use of informal organisation: Managers should identify the informal groups within the formal organisation and use them for effective directing.
  7. Leadership: Managers should use good leadership to influence subordinates positively without causing dissatisfaction.
  8. Follow through: Giving an order is not enough. Managers should follow up, check whether it is being carried out, solve problems and modify instructions if needed.
🧠 Memory trick: "My Happy Uncle Always Makes Indian Lemon Fizz"
Maximum contribution, Harmony, Unity of command, Appropriateness, Managerial communication, Informal organisation, Leadership, Follow through

8 marks9. Maslow's Need Hierarchy Theory (with diagram)

Motivation is the process of stimulating people to act to accomplish desired goals.
Abraham Maslow (psychologist, 1943) said that every human being has a hierarchy of five needs, and behaviour is driven by the need that is not yet satisfied.
Draw this triangle in the exam with 5 layers, from Physiological at the bottom to Self-actualisation at the top.

The Five Needs

  1. Basic Physiological Needs: The most basic needs: hunger, thirst, shelter, sleep. At work: basic salary.
  2. Safety / Security Needs: Protection from physical and emotional harm. At work: job security, stable income, pension plans.
  3. Affiliation / Belonging Needs: Affection, acceptance, friendship, a sense of belonging. At work: cordial relations with colleagues.
  4. Esteem Needs: Self-respect, autonomy, status, recognition, attention. At work: job title, recognition.
  5. Self-Actualisation Needs: The highest need, the drive to become what one is capable of becoming: growth, self-fulfilment, achievement of goals.

Assumptions of the Theory

  1. People's behaviour is based on their needs.
  2. Needs are in a hierarchical order, from basic to higher needs.
  3. A satisfied need no longer motivates. Only the next higher need can.
  4. A person moves to the next level only when the lower need is satisfied.

Relevance: Some people question his classification, but the theory is still useful. It tells managers to identify each employee's need level and motivate accordingly.

  • "He wants a bigger designation and recognition" → Esteem need
  • "She wants to reach her full potential and achieve her dream project" → Self-actualisation

8 marks10. Financial and Non-Financial Incentives (4 + 4)

Incentive means all measures used to motivate people to improve performance.
Financial = in direct money form or measurable in money. Non-financial = gives psychological, social and emotional satisfaction.

💰 Financial Incentives

  1. Pay and allowances: Basic pay, dearness allowance and other allowances, with yearly increments. The basic monetary incentive.
  2. Productivity-linked wage incentives: Wages are linked to increases in productivity, at individual or group level.
  3. Bonus: An incentive paid over and above wages or salary.
  4. Profit sharing: Employees get a share in the profits, which motivates them to increase profits.
  5. Co-partnership / Stock option: Employees are offered company shares below market price. This creates a feeling of ownership (e.g. Infosys).
  6. Retirement benefits: Provident fund, pension and gratuity give financial security after retirement.
  7. Perquisites: Car allowance, housing, medical aid, children's education, etc., over and above salary.

🌟 Non-Financial Incentives

  1. Status: The ranking of positions (authority, prestige, perks). Satisfies esteem and social needs.
  2. Organisational climate: Characteristics such as individual autonomy, reward orientation, consideration for employees and risk-taking, which make an organisation a good place to work.
  3. Career advancement opportunity: Skill development and a sound promotion policy. Promotion "works as a tonic".
  4. Job enrichment: Designing jobs with more variety, higher skills, autonomy and responsibility, so the job itself motivates.
  5. Employee recognition programmes: Appreciating good work, e.g. congratulating, notice-board display, awards, mementos, rewarding suggestions.
  6. Job security: Stability of job and income, so employees work with zeal. (Risk: complacency.)
  7. Employee participation: Involving employees in decisions through joint management committees, work committees and canteen committees.
  8. Employee empowerment: Giving more autonomy and powers to subordinates, so they feel their jobs are important.
🧠 Quick test: If you can put a ₹ figure on it, it is financial. If it is about feeling respected, trusted or growing, it is non-financial.
Promotion involves extra pay, but it counts as non-financial because status and growth matter more than the money.
📌 Case lines
  • "Hostel provides medical aid and free education to employees' children" → Perquisites (financial). Two more of the same category: bonus, profit sharing.
  • "Employees offered shares at less than market price" → Co-partnership / stock option
  • "Employee of the Month photo on the notice board" → Employee recognition
  • "Workers' committee decides canteen menu" → Employee participation
✍️ Explain any 4 financial and 4 non-financial incentives. (8 marks) Financial: pay and allowances, bonus, profit sharing, co-partnership/stock option (or retirement benefits, perquisites). Non-financial: status, career advancement, job enrichment, employee recognition (or job security, participation, empowerment). Write 1–2 lines each.

8 marks11. Ways to Improve Communication Effectiveness

Communication is the process of exchanging ideas, views, facts and feelings between two or more persons to create common understanding. (From the Latin word 'communis', which means "common".)
  1. Clarify the ideas before communication: The manager should first understand the problem fully, analyse it and state it clearly.
  2. Communicate according to the needs of the receiver: Adjust the message to the receiver's education and level of understanding.
  3. Consult others before communicating: Involving subordinates in planning the message gains ready acceptance and willing cooperation.
  4. Be aware of language, tone and content: Use understandable language that does not hurt feelings. The message should encourage a response.
  5. Convey things of help and value to listeners: Relate the message to the listeners' interests and needs so they respond.
  6. Ensure proper feedback: Ask questions and encourage the receiver to respond, to confirm the message was understood.
  7. Communicate for present as well as future: Messages should meet present needs and also support the future goals of the enterprise.
  8. Follow up communications: Regularly review whether instructions are being followed and remove any hurdles.
  9. Be a good listener: Patient, attentive listening solves half the problems. Show interest in what subordinates say.
🧠 Memory trick: Think of the 3 stages of talking to someone:
Before you speak: Clarify, know the Receiver, Consult.
While you speak: Language and tone, Value to listener, Present and future.
After you speak: Feedback, Follow up, Listen.
📌 Case lines

Chapter 1 · Nature and Significance of Management

Management is the process of getting things done with the aim of achieving goals effectively (completing the task) and efficiently (at minimum cost).

8 marks12. Characteristics of Management

  1. Management is a goal-oriented process: Every organisation has goals, and management unites people's efforts towards them. E.g. a retail store aims to increase sales, while the Spastics Society of India aims to educate children with special needs.
  2. Management is all pervasive: It is needed in all organisations (economic, social, political) and in all countries. A petrol pump needs management as much as a hospital. What managers do is the same everywhere. How they do it differs with culture.
  3. Management is multidimensional: It has 3 dimensions:
    • Management of work: turning work into goals and assigning the means to achieve them.
    • Management of people: dealing with employees as individuals and as groups, making their strengths effective and weaknesses irrelevant.
    • Management of operations: managing the production process that turns inputs into outputs.
  4. Management is a continuous process: Planning, organising, staffing, directing and controlling go on simultaneously and all the time.
  5. Management is a group activity: People with different needs work together towards a common goal. This requires teamwork and coordination, while also letting members grow.
  6. Management is a dynamic function: It adapts to the changing environment (social, economic, political). E.g. McDonald's changed its menu for India.
  7. Management is an intangible force: It cannot be seen, but its presence is felt: targets are met, employees are satisfied, and there is order instead of chaos.
⚠️ The textbook summary says "tangible force". That is a printing mistake. Always write intangible force.

8 marks13. Objectives of Management

Management has three types of objectives: Organisational, Social and Personal.

  1. Organisational Objectives: Using human and material resources to the maximum advantage, considering all stakeholders (shareholders, employees, customers, government). The 3 economic objectives are:
    • Survival: The basic objective. The business must earn enough revenue to cover its costs.
    • Profit: Mere survival is not enough. Profit is an incentive for continued operation and covers costs and risks.
    • Growth: The business must grow in the long run. Growth is measured by sales, number of employees, number of products, capital investment, etc.
  2. Social Objectives: Creating benefit for society, e.g. using environment-friendly methods of production, giving jobs to disadvantaged sections, and providing schools and healthcare for the community. (Example: ITC's e-Choupal for farmers.)
  3. Personal Objectives: Employees join to satisfy their needs: financial (salary, perks), social (peer recognition) and higher-level (personal growth). Management must reconcile personal goals with organisational goals for harmony.
🧠 Memory trick: "O-S-P" (Organisational, Social, Personal). The organisational objectives are "SPG": Survival → Profit → Growth (like a ladder).
📌 Case lines
  • "Indian Railways' solar train saves 21,000 litres of diesel and ₹12 lakh a year" → Social objective (environment) + Organisational objective (cost saving / profit)
  • "Company runs a free school for villagers" → Social objective
  • "Employees get good salaries and training for career growth" → Personal objective

8 marks2 marks14. Coordination: Meaning and Features

Coordination is the process by which a manager synchronises the activities of different departments to achieve a common goal.
It is called the essence of management. It is not a separate function but the force that binds all functions, "like a thread in a garland". It begins at planning and continues till controlling.

Features of Coordination

  1. Coordination integrates group efforts: It unites diverse interests into purposeful work and gives a common focus, so performance goes as planned.
  2. Coordination ensures unity of action: It acts as a binding force between departments so all actions aim at organisational goals. E.g. production and sales match production to market demand.
  3. Coordination is a continuous process: It is not a one-time function. It starts at planning and goes on till controlling.
  4. Coordination is an all-pervasive function: It is needed at all levels and in all departments because their activities depend on each other (purchase → production → sales).
  5. Coordination is the responsibility of all managers: Top, middle and operational managers all coordinate: top with subordinates, middle with both top and first-line, operational with workers.
  6. Coordination is a deliberate function: A manager coordinates consciously. Even willing cooperation needs coordination to give it direction.
⭐ Useful line: "Cooperation without coordination may lead to wasted effort. Coordination without cooperation may lead to dissatisfaction among employees."
🧠 Memory trick: "I Use a C-A-R-D"
Integrates, Unity of action, Continuous, All pervasive, Responsibility of all, Deliberate

  • "Firm plans well and has good structure, but plans aren't followed, causing confusion and duplication" → Remedy: coordination
  • "Mumbai Dabbawallas deliver on time through linked groups" → Excellence through coordination

8 marks15. Levels of Management (with diagram)

The authority–responsibility relationship creates a hierarchy of superiors and subordinates. Generally there are three levels.

Draw this 3-layer pyramid and add 2–3 functions beside each level.
  1. Top Management: Senior-most executives: Chairman, CEO, COO, President, Vice-President, CFO. Functions:
    • Integrate diverse elements and coordinate the activities of different departments.
    • Analyse the business environment and its effect on the firm.
    • Formulate overall goals and strategies.
    • Responsible for the welfare and survival of the organisation and its impact on society.
  2. Middle Management: The link between top and lower levels. Division heads, e.g. production manager. Functions:
    • Interpret the policies framed by top management.
    • Ensure their department has the necessary personnel.
    • Assign duties and responsibilities.
    • Motivate staff to achieve objectives.
    • Cooperate with other departments. They are responsible for the activities of first-line managers.
  3. Supervisory / Operational Management: Foremen and supervisors. Functions:
    • Directly oversee the efforts of the workforce.
    • Pass on middle management's instructions to workers.
    • Maintain quality of output, minimise wastage, maintain safety standards.
    • Their authority is limited by the plans of top management.

  • "Ritu is manager of the northern division" → Middle management
  • "He decides the company's 5-year strategy" → Top management
  • "Foreman checks wastage and safety on the shop floor" → Supervisory level

8 marks16. Management as a Series of Functions

All managers perform five interrelated and continuous functions. It is often hard to say where one ends and the next begins. Coordination binds them together.

Planning→ Organising→ Staffing→ Directing→ Controlling↺ Coordination binds all
  1. Planning: Deciding in advance what is to be done and who is to do it: setting goals and ways to achieve them efficiently and effectively. It cannot prevent problems, but it can predict them and prepare contingency plans.
  2. Organising: Assigning duties, grouping tasks, establishing authority and allocating resources to carry out the plan. It decides who will do a task, where and when.
  3. Staffing: Finding the right people for the right job at the right time and place. It includes recruitment, selection, placement and training (the human resource function).
  4. Directing: Leading, influencing and motivating employees to perform their tasks. Includes motivation, leadership, communication and supervision.
  5. Controlling: Monitoring performance towards goals: setting standards, measuring actual performance, comparing it with standards and taking corrective action.
🧠 Memory trick: "Please Organise Some Delicious Cake" = POSDC
📌 Case lines
  • "Smita decides in June how many Diwali candles to make" → Planning
  • "She hires extra workers for the festive season" → Staffing
  • "She compares actual output with the target and fixes the gap" → Controlling
